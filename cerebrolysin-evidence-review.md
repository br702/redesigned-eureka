# Cerebrolysin: An Evidence Review

As of 2 October 2026. Companion copy of the Claude Doc at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/8bb9771d-5eb1-4697-973f-f44ac16fd7c8

## Bottom line

Cerebrolysin is a prescription injectable made from pig brain, approved in roughly 50 countries but not in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom or most of Western Europe [1,78]. After five decades of use and dozens of trials, independent systematic reviews find no convincing clinical benefit for its main indication, acute ischaemic stroke, and a moderate-certainty signal of more non-fatal serious adverse events [1,2,3]. Almost every positive trial and meta-analysis was funded, supplied or analysed by the manufacturer, EVER Neuro Pharma (formerly Ebewe) [1,8,9,26]. A large part of the animal literature that underpins its "neurotrophic" story has been retracted or placed under expression of concern since 2024 [79-86].

| Claim | Status | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduces death after ischaemic stroke | Not supported. RR 0.96 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.41), 6 RCTs, 1689 patients, moderate certainty | Cochrane 2023 [1] |
| Improves long-term function (modified Rankin Scale) after stroke | Not shown in the one large trial (CASTA, n=1070, neutral primary endpoint); positive only in industry pooled analyses of early NIHSS change | CASTA [5]; Bornstein 2018 [9]; Wang 2017 [14] |
| Speeds early neurological recovery after stroke (NIHSS at day 21 to 30) | Probable but modest, and almost entirely from manufacturer-run trials and pooled analyses; NNT about 8 for a 4-point NIHSS gain | Bornstein 2018 [9]; Patel 2025 [15] |
| Improves outcome after moderate to severe traumatic brain injury | Speculative. Two small RCTs (n=46 and n=139) with a composite endpoint; pooled effect small (SMD about 0.3); the multicentre trial was stopped for poor recruitment | CAPTAIN I and II [24,25,26]; registry NCT01606111 [88] |
| Improves cognition in vascular dementia | Probable small short-term effect of uncertain clinical value (SMD 0.36, very low certainty; independent pooled SMD 0.16) | Cochrane 2019 [35]; Alsulaimani 2021 [38] |
| Improves cognition in Alzheimer's disease | Probable small short-term effect at 4 weeks (SMD about 0.4) that was not significant at 6 months; no trial longer than 28 weeks | Gauthier 2015 [39]; Ruether 2001 [41] |
| Enhances cognition in healthy people | Unknown. No randomised trial of the injectable in healthy adults exists; one tiny uncontrolled oral single-dose study | Alvarez 2000 [63] |
| Safe | Common adverse events similar to placebo; non-fatal serious adverse events roughly doubled in stroke trials (3.0% vs 1.2%, NNH about 56); rare anaphylaxis | Cochrane 2017 and 2023 [1,3]; Trimmel 2024 [76] |

What is established: the drug is generally well tolerated in supervised hospital use, and no large trial has shown a mortality benefit. What is probable: a small acceleration of early recovery scores in stroke and small short-term cognitive gains in dementia, both of uncertain durability and real-world meaning. What is speculative: benefit in traumatic brain injury, haemorrhage, cerebral palsy, spinal cord disease, depression and autism. What is unknown: pharmacokinetics in humans, which peptides are active, whether grey-market intramuscular self-injection does anything at all, and the true frequency of serious allergic reactions.

## What Cerebrolysin is

Cerebrolysin is a sterile aqueous solution of low-molecular-weight peptides and free amino acids produced by standardised enzymatic digestion of purified pig brain protein [1,67]. The manufacturer states that each millilitre contains 215.2 mg of Cerebrolysin concentrate with sodium hydroxide and water for injection as the only excipients, and that roughly 15 to 25 percent of the mass is peptide and the rest free amino acids [54,89]. It is supplied in 1, 5, 10 and 20 mL ampoules or vials for intramuscular injection or intravenous infusion [89,90]. There is no oral product; a weaker oral derivative, N-PEP-12, was tested once and is not the same substance [64].

**Who makes it.** EVER Neuro Pharma GmbH, Unterach, Austria (formerly Ebewe Pharma) holds the marketing authorisations [3,89]. Indian generics labelled "cerebroprotein hydrolysate" and Russian brain-hydrolysate products such as Cortexin and Cerebrolysat are different preparations with their own, thinner, evidence; the 2023 Cochrane update treated Cortexin as a "Cerebrolysin-like agent" [1]. An Internet-purchased sample analysed by Ghent University contained 638 identifiable peptides, mostly fragments of tubulin, actin and myelin basic protein [65].

**Regulatory status.** The product is not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, is not listed on DailyMed, has no European Medicines Agency central authorisation, and is not licensed in the UK [78]. National approvals exist in Austria, Germany, Poland, Croatia, Russia, China, South Korea, Vietnam and other Eastern European and Asian markets, with labelled indications of ischaemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, vascular dementia and Alzheimer-type dementia [78,89]. Two 2024 to 2026 reviews describe it as licensed in "over 50 countries" [78]. Labelled contraindications are hypersensitivity, epilepsy, and severe renal impairment [89].

**How it is sold.** In licensed markets it is a prescription product given in hospitals or clinics by infusion. A 5-pack of 10 mL ampoules listed at Polish pharmacies for about 180 to 190 PLN in 2025, roughly 45 to 50 USD [90]. It is also widely resold online to US and UK buyers without prescription, where it is promoted as a "nootropic peptide" for intramuscular self-injection [91]. That use has no regulatory cover, no quality assurance for the product in hand, and no clinical trial behind it.

**Evidence tier for this section:** regulatory and manufacturer documents, one independent analytical chemistry study [65], and narrative reviews. The composition claims are the manufacturer's; the one independent assay found no fragments of the neurotrophic factors (NGF, BDNF, GDNF, CNTF) the product is said to mimic [65].

## Proposed mechanism and pharmacokinetics

The marketing claim is that Cerebrolysin's peptides "mimic the action of endogenous neurotrophic factors" such as nerve growth factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and so protect neurons, reduce amyloid and tau pathology, and promote neurogenesis and synaptic repair [67,68]. Every link in that chain rests on in-vitro or animal work, much of it from two laboratories whose Cerebrolysin papers have since been retracted.

| Claim | Evidence tier | What was found | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protects cultured neurons from glutamate and oxygen-glucose deprivation | In vitro | Dose-dependent rescue, even with delayed addition up to 48 to 96 h [72] | Manufacturer-affiliated authors; concentrations not translatable to plasma levels |
| Reduces amyloid burden and improves water-maze performance in APP transgenic mice | Animal | 27 to 43 percent amyloid reduction; behavioural gains [69,70] | Rockenstein and Masliah laboratory; two Cerebrolysin papers from this group retracted in 2025 and 2026 for image manipulation, one under expression of concern [79,80,81,83]; NIH found research misconduct by Masliah in 2024 [87] |
| Improves recovery in rat embolic stroke when started up to 48 h after occlusion | Animal | Better behavioural scores, no change in infarct volume or mortality [71] | Industry-supported; the human stroke trials did not reproduce functional benefit |
| Promotes remyelination and neurogenesis; "nanowired" delivery is superior | Animal | Dozens of papers from the Sharma group, Uppsala | Three Cerebrolysin papers retracted 2025 to 2026 for overlapping figure panels; authors disagreed with retraction [84,85,86] |
| Raises serum BDNF, lowers TNF-alpha and raises IGF-1 in treated Alzheimer patients | Human biomarker (secondary analyses of industry RCTs) | Dose-related changes reported [73,74] | Serum levels are not brain levels; biomarker shifts did not translate into 6-month cognitive benefit in the same trial series [39] |
| Contains enkephalins, orexin and galanin fragments that explain mood effects | Analytical chemistry plus bioinformatics | Russian mass-spectrometry study of the "light" fraction reports 14,635 peptides [66] | An independent Belgian assay of an Internet-bought sample found 638 peptides, mostly tubulin, actin and myelin basic protein, and no neurotrophic-factor fragments [65] |

**Pharmacokinetics.** There are no published human pharmacokinetic studies. The product is a mixture of thousands of peptides without a defined active ingredient, so plasma concentration, half-life, brain penetration and dose-exposure relationships have never been characterised in people. Claims that the peptides "cross the blood-brain barrier" come from animal tracer work and from narrative reviews [68]. Intact oral absorption of peptides is not expected, which is why the licensed product is injectable only; the one oral single-dose study in 2000 was uncontrolled and used the infusion solution by mouth [63].

**Assessment.** Mechanism is speculative. The preclinical base is large but concentrated in industry-linked groups, and the part of it that has been scrutinised has not held up. A plausible mechanism is not required for a drug to work, but it does mean the clinical trials have to carry the whole weight, and they do not.

## Acute ischaemic stroke and stroke recovery

This is the best-studied indication and the clearest case of independent and industry-run syntheses reaching opposite conclusions from overlapping trials.

**Independent systematic review (highest tier).** The Cochrane review, first published in 2010 and updated four times, most recently in 2023, pooled seven randomised trials of Cerebrolysin or the related product Cortexin started within 48 hours of stroke, 1773 participants in Austria, Croatia, Czechia, Hungary, Russia, Slovakia, Slovenia, China, Hong Kong, Iran, Myanmar and South Korea [1,2]. Findings:

- All-cause death: RR 0.96 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.41), 6 trials, 1689 patients, moderate certainty. No benefit [1].
- Death or dependence at end of follow-up, the standard stroke outcome: not reported in a usable form by any included trial [1,2].
- Any serious adverse event: RR 1.16 (0.81 to 1.66), moderate certainty [1].
- Non-fatal serious adverse events: RR 2.39 (1.10 to 5.23), 3 trials, 1335 patients, moderate certainty; RR 2.87 in the 30 mL x 10 day subgroup [1]. In absolute terms from the 2017 analysis: 20 of 667 on Cerebrolysin versus 8 of 668 on placebo, 3.0% versus 1.2%, a number needed to harm of about 56 [3].
- Any adverse event: RR 1.03 (0.92 to 1.14), low certainty [1].

The reviewers judged selective-reporting risk unclear in every trial, attrition risk high in four of seven, and noted that the manufacturer supplied drug, placebo, randomisation codes, grants or statisticians to the three multicentre trials [1]. A 2017 Stroke editorial summarised the position as "evidence again fails to support a neuroprotectant" [22]; the statisticians who ran the industry analyses replied [23].

**Industry-led meta-analyses (lower tier for independence).** A pooled analysis of nine RCTs (1879 patients) using individual patient data supplied by the company reported superiority on NIHSS at day 21 to 30 (Mann-Whitney 0.60) and a number needed to treat of 7.7 (95% CI 5.2 to 15.0) for a clinically relevant 4-point NIHSS change; the day-90 mRS benefit was shown only in a post hoc moderate-to-severe subgroup of 314 patients [9]. The two CARS trials pooled gave an NNT of 7.1 (4 to 22) for early NIHSS response [8]. A 2021 safety meta-analysis of 12 RCTs by authors affiliated with the main Romanian trial site found no excess of serious adverse events [16]. The search strategy for the nine-trial analysis was assisted by the manufacturer, risk of bias was assessed by the company's contract statisticians, and no review protocol was registered [9].

**Independent meta-analyses using conventional outcomes.** Two 2017 Chinese meta-analyses of six and seven RCTs found no effect on day-90 mRS response (RR 1.33, 0.79 to 2.24), NIHSS response, or Barthel Index, and no safety difference [13,14]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (2884 patients) found a 1.39-point greater NIHSS improvement (0.53 to 2.25) but no significant effect on functional independence (RR 1.31, 0.90 to 1.91) [15]. A 2024 network meta-analysis ranked Cerebrolysin below edaravone and butylphthalide and found a higher adverse-event rate than control [92].

**Observational and registry data (lowest clinical tier).** The company-run C-REGS2 effectiveness registry in 16 countries (2018 to 2024) and a Polish propensity-matched thrombectomy cohort both report large benefits, for example an adjusted odds ratio of 6.1 for 12-month independence after thrombectomy [19,20]. These are non-randomised, open-label and in one case compared with historical controls; a meta-analysis of three such cohorts (294 patients) reports a 56 percent relative increase in good outcome but the authors concede the design cannot establish causation [21].

**Reading of the evidence.** Established: Cerebrolysin does not reduce death after ischaemic stroke, and the one adequately powered trial for function was neutral. Probable: a small acceleration of early NIHSS improvement (about 1 to 1.5 points over 3 to 4 weeks) in trials run by or with the manufacturer. Unknown: whether that early difference matters to patients, since death or dependence was never properly measured. The repeated pattern of neutral primary endpoints rescued by post hoc subgroups, nonparametric composite statistics and individual-patient-data pooling by the sponsor's statisticians is the main reason independent reviewers remain unconvinced [1,22].

## Traumatic brain injury

Cerebrolysin is labelled for "craniocerebral trauma" in its licensed markets [89], but the randomised evidence is two small trials and one pooled analysis, all run with the manufacturer.

| Study | Design | n | Regimen | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CAPTAIN I, Poon [24,27] | Multicentre RCT, Asia-Pacific; registered as NCT01606111 and terminated for poor patient recruitment [88] | 46 | 50 mL/day IV x 10 d, then two cycles of 10 mL/day x 10 d | Primary composite of 14 scales missed significance in the intention-to-treat population (p<0.1, MW 0.63); significant per protocol |
| CAPTAIN II, Muresanu [25] | Single-centre RCT, Cluj-Napoca, Romania | 139 analysed | Same regimen | Composite of 13 scales favoured Cerebrolysin at day 90, MW 0.59 (0.52 to 0.66), p=0.012 |
| CAPTAIN pooled, Vester [26] | Prospective meta-analysis of the two | 185 | Same | MW 0.60, SMD 0.31 to 0.34, OR about 1.7 at day 30 and 90; described by the authors as "small-to-medium" |
| Chen [31] | Pilot RCT, mild TBI, Taiwan | 32 | 30 mL/day IV x 5 d | Greater change on a cognitive screening instrument at 12 weeks (21.0 vs 7.6 points, p=0.046) |
| CAPTAIN-rTMS, Verisezan Rosu [32] | Three-arm RCT with transcranial magnetic stimulation | 93 | Cerebrolysin plus rTMS, Cerebrolysin plus sham, placebo plus sham | No significant difference on the primary cognitive composites at 3 or 6 months |

**Meta-analyses.** A 2019 meta-analysis of five studies (5685 participants) found better Glasgow Outcome Scale scores (SMD 0.30, 0.18 to 0.42) but was driven by retrospective cohorts with heterogeneity near 90 percent and explicitly noted "a lack of clinical trials" [28]. A 2023 meta-analysis of ten mostly observational studies (8749 patients) reported improved Glasgow scores but no effect on mortality or length of stay [29]. An earlier pooled estimate of favourable outcome, OR 3.0, rested on 112 treated patients [30].

**Observational data.** Multicentre retrospective cohorts [33,34] and a prospective single-blind Thai cohort [93] report better early outcomes and in one case much higher 6-month survival (59% versus 28%). Allocation in these studies was by physician choice; the survival difference is implausibly large for a drug with no mortality effect in 1700 stroke patients and is best read as confounding by indication.

**Reading of the evidence.** Speculative. The only multicentre RCT enrolled only 46 patients before termination for poor recruitment and missed its primary endpoint. The positive trial was single-centre, 139 patients, with a bespoke composite endpoint analysed by the sponsor's statistical contractor. No trial has shown an effect on the standard TBI outcomes of mortality or dichotomised Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended. A 2025 review of neuroprotection after TBI lists Cerebrolysin among agents still "being evaluated" rather than among anything proven [94].

## Dementia: Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia

Dementia is where Cerebrolysin's trial record is longest, and where the gap between "statistically significant" and "clinically meaningful" is widest. All trials are short (4 to 28 weeks), all used repeated intravenous infusion cycles, and every one with disclosed funding was supported by Ebewe or EVER [35,39].

**Vascular dementia, independent review (highest tier).** The Cochrane review, updated in 2019, found six RCTs with 597 participants, three in China, one in Russia, one in Romania, all industry-supported where funding was stated; no new trial had appeared since 2013 [35,36]. Pooled cognition (MMSE or ADAS-cog+, three trials, 420 people) favoured Cerebrolysin, SMD 0.36 (0.13 to 0.58), very low certainty. Global response rate was higher, RR 2.69 (1.82 to 3.98), very low certainty. Adverse events did not differ. The authors' conclusion: "if there are benefits of Cerebrolysin, the effects may be too small to be clinically meaningful" [35]. The largest single trial (Guekht, n=242, 20 mL/day in two cycles over 24 weeks) reported a 6.2-point greater ADAS-cog+ improvement than placebo and a combined response rate of 67.5% versus 27.0% [37], numbers out of line with every other trial and with the pooled estimate.

**Alzheimer's disease.** No Cochrane review exists. Trials and syntheses:

**Mild cognitive impairment and prevention.** Two Russian open-label studies (n=110 and n=100) report that yearly 4-week infusion courses slowed progression to dementia over 3 years [47,48]. Neither was blinded or placebo-controlled; both are from a single research group. There is no controlled evidence that Cerebrolysin prevents dementia.

**Scale of effect for context.** An SMD of 0.16 to 0.40 corresponds to roughly 1 to 2.5 ADAS-cog points over 4 to 16 weeks. That is the size of a symptomatic effect, not disease modification, and no Cerebrolysin trial ran long enough to measure slowing of decline at all.

**Reading of the evidence.** Probable small, short-lived improvement in cognition and clinician global impression at 4 to 16 weeks in mild to moderate dementia, from industry-supported trials of 50 to 280 patients. No evidence beyond 28 weeks, no evidence on institutionalisation, carer burden or quality of life, and no dose-response. Independent pooled estimates are about half the size of industry ones. It is not a licensed dementia treatment in the United States, the United Kingdom or under central European Union authorisation [78].

## Other indications

Cerebrolysin has been tried in almost every neurological and psychiatric condition. Outside stroke, TBI and dementia, the evidence is single small trials, usually unblinded, usually from one country or one group, and none has been replicated.

**Reading of the evidence.** Nothing here rises above speculative. The pattern of very large effects in small unblinded trials from single centres, with no independent replication, is the signature of bias rather than of a powerful drug. Negative trials exist and are informative: subarachnoid haemorrhage, multiple sclerosis and schizophrenia all failed on their primary endpoints [50,53,60].

## Safety

In supervised hospital use Cerebrolysin is well tolerated, with common adverse events at placebo rates. The two genuine safety signals are a doubling of non-fatal serious adverse events in acute stroke trials and rare anaphylaxis.

**Common adverse events.** Across stroke RCTs the proportion of patients with any adverse event was identical to placebo (RR 1.03, 0.92 to 1.14, 1607 patients) [1]. In the pooled CARS trials 46.0% of Cerebrolysin and 41.5% of placebo patients had at least one event, mostly mild; the only events above 5 percent were urinary tract infection and depression, both also common on placebo [8]. In dementia trials the most frequent drug-attributed events were dizziness or vertigo, headache, agitation, feeling hot, sweating and weight loss [42,75,77]. Rapid injection can cause a sensation of heat and local pain [89].

**Serious adverse events.** The Cochrane stroke review found no difference in total or fatal serious adverse events, but non-fatal serious adverse events were more frequent: RR 2.39 (1.10 to 5.23) in 2023, RR 2.15 (1.01 to 4.55) in 2020, and 20 of 667 versus 8 of 668 in the 2017 analysis [1,2,3]. The excess was concentrated in the 30 mL for 10 days regimen (RR 2.87, 1.24 to 6.69) [1]. The events were not characterised by type, the finding is based on three trials, and the manufacturer-linked meta-analysis of twelve trials did not reproduce it [16]. It should be treated as a real, moderate-certainty signal of unknown mechanism, with an absolute excess of about 1.8 per 100 treated patients, a number needed to harm near 56.

**Hypersensitivity and anaphylaxis.** Cerebrolysin is a foreign-protein hydrolysate given intravenously, so allergic reactions are expected. A 2024 Austrian case report documents laboratory-confirmed, life-threatening anaphylaxis in an 85-year-old stroke patient and notes that only rare cases have been published [76]. Hypersensitivity is a labelled contraindication [89]. No pharmacovigilance rate is published; the true frequency is unknown.

**Seizures and epilepsy.** Epilepsy is a labelled contraindication [89]. The basis appears to be theoretical concern about excitatory peptides and clinical tradition rather than trial data; none of the RCTs reported an excess of seizures, but none was designed to detect one.

**Renal impairment.** Severe renal impairment is a labelled contraindication, presumably because the product is a concentrated amino-acid and peptide load [89]. No pharmacokinetic study exists to quantify the risk.

**Prion and infection risk.** The product is made from pig brain. Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy has not been reported in pigs under natural conditions, and the manufacturer describes controlled sourcing and processing; no case of transmitted infection has been reported in five decades of use [1,75]. The theoretical risk is not zero and cannot be ruled out for product of unverified origin bought online.

**Injection practice outside clinics.** Grey-market users typically self-inject 5 to 10 mL intramuscularly [91]. Risks specific to that practice are unsterile product, counterfeit or degraded ampoules, injection-site infection, and anaphylaxis without medical help at hand. None of the trials studied intramuscular dosing at these volumes.

**Pregnancy and lactation.** No human data. The manufacturer advises use only after careful risk-benefit assessment [89].

**Drug interactions.** None established. Co-administration with alteplase, donepezil and rivastigmine in trials did not produce safety signals [10,45,75]. Antidepressants and MAO inhibitors are listed as theoretical interactions in some national labels; the basis is not documented in the trial literature.

**Hepatotoxicity.** The US National Library of Medicine's LiverTox database has no entry for Cerebrolysin, and no liver injury signal appears in the trials. Hepatic safety is simply unstudied rather than demonstrated.

## Conflicts of interest, funding and research integrity

The single most important fact for interpreting this literature is that the positive clinical evidence and the negative independent syntheses were produced by different people with different relationships to the manufacturer.

**Trial funding.** Cochrane reviewers documented that EVER Neuro Pharma or its predecessor Ebewe supported the multicentre stroke trials "either totally, or by providing Cerebrolysin and placebo, randomisation codes, research grants, or statisticians" [1,3]. CASTA, CARS-1 and CARS-2, CAPTAIN I and II, and the Alzheimer's trials of Ruether, Alvarez and Panisset all list manufacturer support [5,7,8,24,25,41,43]. In the vascular dementia Cochrane review, every trial that disclosed funding was industry-supported [35]. No large independent, publicly funded RCT of Cerebrolysin has ever been run.

**Analysis and authorship.** The nonparametric "Mann-Whitney" composite statistics used in CARS, CAPTAIN and the pooled stroke analyses were designed and run by idv Data Analysis and Study Planning, a German contract firm whose staff are co-authors on the trials and the meta-analyses and who wrote the letter defending Cerebrolysin against the Cochrane findings [8,9,23,24,26]. The nine-trial stroke meta-analysis states that the manufacturer helped identify sources and that no protocol was registered [9]. The 12-trial safety meta-analysis and several TBI secondary analyses come from the Cluj-Napoca group that ran CAPTAIN II, CARS and ESCAS and publishes cost-effectiveness analyses for the product [16,18,25].

**Independent reviews.** Cochrane Russia (stroke, five updates), West China Hospital (vascular dementia), the Glasgow group (all dementia), two Chinese stroke meta-analyses and the 2025 14-trial meta-analysis all reached null or marginal conclusions on patient-relevant endpoints [1,13,14,15,35,38]. The company's critique of Cochrane rests on the argument that mortality is an insensitive endpoint and that early NIHSS change should count [9,23]; Cochrane's counter is that no trial reported death or dependence, the outcome regulators require, and that the sponsor controlled which outcomes were published [1,22].

In September 2024 the US National Institutes of Health announced that an investigation had found research misconduct by Eliezer Masliah, then director of the neuroscience division of the National Institute on Aging, after a Science investigation identified apparent image manipulation in 132 of his papers [87]. Masliah co-authored the manufacturer's main pharmacology review of Cerebrolysin [67] and the transgenic-mouse series that is cited in nearly every clinical paper as the mechanistic rationale [69,70]. The Uppsala group's retracted papers include several co-authored with the lead investigator of the CAPTAIN and CARS trials [84,85]. None of the clinical trials has been retracted, and no finding of misconduct applies to them. But the preclinical foundation that reviews and marketing materials cite has been materially weakened, and more scrutiny of this body of work is likely.

**Publication and reporting bias.** Cochrane rated selective reporting "unclear" in all stroke trials because protocols were unavailable or outcomes differed from registrations [1]. CARS-2, a negative-leaning trial, was never published on its own and appears only inside a pooled analysis [8]. The CAPTAIN multicentre trial was stopped for recruitment and reported with 46 patients [24,88]. Russian-language journals carry a large volume of small positive studies, often with manufacturer acknowledgement, that do not reach Western databases.

**Reading.** The conflict of interest here is not a formality. The direction and size of the reported effect track who did the analysis. That does not prove the drug is inert, but it means the burden of proof has not been met by anyone without a stake in the answer.

## Dosing: what the trials used versus what is sold

Every trial that reported an effect used daily intravenous infusion of 10 to 50 mL, diluted in 100 to 250 mL saline over 15 to 60 minutes, in courses of 10 to 21 consecutive days, started within hours to days of an acute event or given as repeated 4-week cycles in dementia [5,7,24,41,43]. The grey-market practice of 5 to 10 mL intramuscularly has never been tested in a randomised trial.

**Manufacturer's labelled dosing.** The product information recommends 10 to 50 mL per day by slow intravenous infusion for 10 to 20 days, with up to 5 mL allowed intramuscularly and up to 10 mL as a slow intravenous push; repeat courses are suggested two to four times a year for chronic conditions [89,90]. These recommendations match the trial regimens for stroke and TBI and are broader than the evidence for dementia.

**Dose-response.** None has been demonstrated. The only dose-ranging RCT found the lowest dose, 10 mL, to be the most effective on cognition and the highest, 60 mL, ineffective on cognition [43]. In stroke, the Cochrane analysis found the serious adverse event excess greatest at 30 mL for 10 days, the standard regimen [1]. A manufacturer-linked analysis argued the opposite, that 50 mL was safest [16]. Neither is a dose-response study.

**Time to effect.** Where effects were reported they appeared within the treatment course: NIHSS at 2 to 21 days in stroke, cognition at 4 weeks in dementia [8,10,41]. Claims of a delayed or cumulative effect from repeated cycles rest on one 28-week Alzheimer's trial and open-label extension data [41,47].

**Cycling.** The 4-weeks-on, 8-weeks-off pattern used in dementia trials is the origin of the "cycle" idea. Transgenic-mouse work from the Masliah group suggested effects persisted about 3 months after stopping [98], but that laboratory's Cerebrolysin papers are now under integrity review [79-83]. In humans, the only test of cycling is Ruether 2001, which showed maintenance to week 28 but did not compare one course with two [41].

**Forms sold versus forms tested.**

- Intravenous infusion of 10 to 50 mL: the only form with controlled evidence of any kind.
- Intramuscular injection of 1 to 5 mL: licensed for convenience; tested only in paediatric trials at 0.1 mL/kg [57,58,59] and in uncontrolled adult case series.
- Grey-market protocols of 5 mL IM daily for 10 to 20 days followed by one to two months off [91]: one sixth to one tenth of the adult trial dose, by a route and in a population never studied. There is no evidence for or against an effect at these doses.
- Oral: no licensed product. A single uncontrolled study gave the infusion solution by mouth once [63]; peptides are not expected to survive digestion. The oral derivative N-PEP-12 is a different, weaker product with one 30-day trial [64].
- "Cerebroprotein hydrolysate" generics, Cortexin, Cerebrolysat: different manufacturing processes, different peptide profiles, essentially no independent trial evidence; Cochrane pooled Cortexin with Cerebrolysin only in 2023 on the basis of one 272-patient trial [1].

## Practical summary

**Who might reasonably consider it.** A patient in a country where Cerebrolysin is licensed, in hospital after an acute ischaemic stroke or moderate to severe head injury, whose treating neurologist offers it as an add-on within a supervised infusion protocol. Even then the honest framing is: it will probably not change your chance of surviving or of being independent at three months, it may speed early recovery scores by a point or two, and it carries a small excess of serious non-fatal events. A patient with mild to moderate Alzheimer's or vascular dementia in such a country could reasonably try one 4-week infusion course expecting, at most, a small and temporary symptomatic effect. In both cases this is a judgement call made with a physician, not a self-directed decision.

**Who should avoid it.**

- Anyone with epilepsy or a seizure history, severe kidney disease, or prior allergic reaction to the product or to porcine material (labelled contraindications) [89].
- Anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding, since there are no human data [89].
- Anyone proposing to self-inject grey-market product. Unverified sterility and origin, anaphylaxis without medical backup, and a dose and route no trial has tested combine to give known risks and no demonstrated benefit [65,76,91].
- Healthy adults seeking cognitive enhancement. No randomised trial of the injectable exists in healthy people. The marketing claim rests on extrapolation from dementia trials whose own effects are small and short-lived [38,63].
- Anyone who would be using it instead of established treatment: thrombolysis or thrombectomy in stroke, structured rehabilitation after stroke or TBI, cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine in dementia, or antidepressants in depression.

**What to discuss with a doctor or pharmacist.**

1. Whether the product is licensed where you live, and whether the clinician has given it before. In the US, UK and most of Western Europe the answer is no, and importing it for personal use is legally uncertain [78].
2. What specific outcome you are hoping to change, over what time frame, and how you will know if it is working. For stroke, the trials measured early NIHSS and arm function, not independence; for dementia, cognition at 4 to 16 weeks.
3. Kidney function, seizure history and any history of allergy, which are the labelled exclusions [89].
4. That it is given by slow intravenous infusion with the ability to treat anaphylaxis, not by intramuscular self-injection [76,89].
5. Interactions are not established but the product has been co-administered with alteplase and cholinesterase inhibitors in trials without new safety signals [10,45].
6. Cost. At licensed-market prices a 10-day course of 30 mL costs roughly 150 to 300 USD in drug alone [90]; grey-market US resale prices are several times higher and buy product of unknown provenance.
7. That the drug's evidence base has moved against it since 2016: four Cochrane updates with null findings, an unresolved serious adverse event signal, and retraction of key laboratory papers in 2025 and 2026 [1,79-86].

**One-paragraph verdict.** Cerebrolysin is a legitimately licensed drug in part of the world, well tolerated in clinics, and supported by a large but industry-dependent trial literature that shows small, early, surrogate-endpoint improvements and nothing on the outcomes that matter most. Independent reviewers have not found convincing benefit, and the preclinical story is less solid than it looked two years ago. For a patient within a licensed system it is a low-value add-on to discuss with a neurologist. For everyone else, and especially for self-injection as a nootropic, the evidence does not support use.
